# Continuous acting, reference roles and perceived age

Use for dialogue/emotional acting, expression continuity, or feedback that a face looks too old or carries the wrong mood. These are empirical directions, not deterministic controls. Preserve the user's target performance and accepted identity.

## A changing performance

- Name the observable sequence rather than repeating an emotion label. An attempt to reassure can briefly lift the mouth corners while the gaze remains strained; the smile can weaken before the lower lids become wet and a tear falls. This is an example, not a compulsory sadness arc. Follow the actual source's order, intensity and pauses.
- Preserve the beginning and completion of important head/hand actions. Looking down, lifting the head, touching the cheek and resting on a hand are different actions; do not substitute one because all look “sad.” Describe only those present in the source or requested change.
- In a two-shot, a listener can make a small gaze adjustment, breathe or shift weight while remaining attentive. Avoid frozen faces, synchronized blinking, repeated nodding or extra lines. Reactions should not steal the speaking character's beat.
- Carry the actual accepted state across a real continuation: gaze, head angle, mouth tension, skin color, tear tracks and contact. Do not inherit a rejected exaggeration. A new day or memory can require a different state; previous crying does not imply ongoing sobbing.
- A short reference image can be calm and serve identity/pose while the video prompt carries the emotional development. Do not mandate extra expression sheets or transition portraits. When an exact starting state matters, preserve it instead of neutralizing it automatically.

## Animation translated into live action

Identify the emotional and interpersonal intention behind a drawn expression. An anime crescent-eye smile can mean joyful attention rather than sustained literal eye closure. When that is the requested interpretation, describe gently lifted cheeks, warm naturally open eyes, a plausible gaze toward the other person, and brief ordinary blinks. Do not change every closed-eye smile: preserve explicit eye closure and the user's approved shot-specific expression.

## Age, style and identity are separate

- A style reference may supply lighting, color, lens character and texture without supplying its face, age or expression. Explicitly separate those roles.
- Use the user's requested visible age and styling rather than relying on a narrative role such as “mother” or “father” to produce the face. Mention the relationship in an image prompt only if it changes visible pose/contact or another necessary detail; it remains valid in the video script.
- When comparing the same character at different life stages, preserve recognizable facial relationships while making the requested age difference readable through appropriate styling and natural facial softness. Do not make everyone an identical age, or force maturity through heavy lines and makeup.
- If a reference repeatedly pulls toward an explicitly rejected age/identity, change the reference strategy within the user's authorized edit instead of adding contradictory adjectives. Do not redesign an accepted face simply because a different look might be prettier.
- Preserve natural skin texture and anatomical proportions; youth does not mean waxy smoothing, enlarged eyes, oversized head or a shrunken body. Beauty and sweetness follow the requested character, not a compulsory smile.

Review the overall image's usefulness first. Keep a broadly usable result and report small remaining issues; do not repeatedly remodel the face or add paid variants without the relevant user authorization.
