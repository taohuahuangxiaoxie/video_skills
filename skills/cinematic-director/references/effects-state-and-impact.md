# Effects: State, Structure and Impact

Use for multi-shot effects continuity, strong luminous effects, or a requested fast impact. These are prompt-design checks derived from observed failures, not guarantees of model fidelity. Apply only the layers and intensity that the approved scene needs.

## Carry the state through the cut

An effect belongs to the scene timeline, not to the prompt that first introduced it. A new segment must not repeat an already-completed charge, formation or summon. Read the previous approved exit state before writing the next entry state.

Use a compact planning row when continuity is at risk; no separate tracking file is required:

| Effect | Entry phase | Spatial anchor and scale | Intensity and environmental response | Changes now | Persists until |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Shield ring | Already complete and fully luminous | Centered behind the actor, several body-heights across | Broad reflected light on the ground and the actor's outline | Inner streams accelerate toward the emitter | The scripted shutdown after impact |

Carry the relevant parts into each shot prompt. Check the planned keyframe against that entry state: an unlit object or half-formed ring contradicts “already at full charge.” Report or correct a material mismatch within the user's authorized scope; do not silently restart the action to match the wrong image.

A close-up can crop an effect without extinguishing it. Where useful, retain an edge, reflected light or atmospheric cue; on the next wide, restore the same world position, scale and phase. Different exposure or framing may change apparent brightness, but should not accidentally imply a loss of power. Brightness may rise, hold or fall when the script calls for it; continuity is not a universal rule of ever-increasing light.

## Separate structure from moving energy

Assign separate verbs to separate components:

- **Persistent structure:** the outer ring, barrier, field or formation. State where it remains and what event ends it.
- **Internal motion:** circulating bands, inward streams or rotating segments. Name what moves, its axis or path, and what stays rigid.
- **Released energy:** the pulse, beam or projectile. Name its source, travel direction and effect on contact.

Avoid a contradiction such as “the entire ring collapses into the emitter” followed by “the ring remains behind the actor.” If persistence is intended, write:

> The completed outer ring holds its diameter and position behind the actor throughout release and impact. Only the inner light streams converge into the emitter. The pulse leaves the emitter while the outer ring remains fully luminous; it fades only at the scripted shutdown.

For a segmented prop, “segments rotate around one straight common axis” preserves its geometry; “the whole object twists” may instead suggest bending. Distinguish intended rigid rotation from deformation.

## Make strong effects visible

Replace “more epic, brighter, stronger” with a few mutually consistent visible results. For a luminous effect, choose the needed layers:

| Layer | What to specify |
|---|---|
| Core and outline | A bright core plus a readable colored edge or silhouette; where detail must survive |
| Bloom and illuminated air | How far the halo spreads, the volume it fills, and what it lights |
| Secondary motion | Dense streams, sparks, rotating bands or pressure ripples with a clear direction and speed |
| Environment | Moving reflections, dust displacement, cloth response or local distortion caused by the effect |

Scale and occupied area matter as much as particle count. A climax already established as intense should open at that level rather than rebuild from isolated sparks. Keep faces and the main action readable; filling the whole frame with featureless white is not a substitute for scale. For non-luminous effects, use the appropriate mass, displacement and occlusion instead of adding glow.

Example of a peak-state continuation:

> The emitter is already saturated with a white-hot core and a gold edge. Broad light fills the mist around the completed ring; dense inner streams race forward. A moving reflection sweeps the paving, and the pressure drives dust and loose cloth away from the source. The actor's face and the ring's outer contour remain readable.

## Give a fast collision a change of state

When the approved beat requires a forceful impact, separate approach, contact, breakthrough or recoil, and aftermath. Give each phase its own speed and visible consequence; do not let a requested decisive hit become a long, evenly moving tug-of-war.

- **Approach:** rapid travel with a readable vector, rather than slow floating light.
- **Contact:** a brief compression or hard contrast flash, optionally a short camera jolt tied to the hit.
- **Resolution:** the specified breakthrough, deflection or recoil changes the image decisively.
- **Aftermath:** the camera recovers and the remaining structures, debris and light settle into the next required state.

Allocate these phases within the user's duration and pacing. A deliberately sustained clash should remain sustained. “One- or two-frame impact” describes an editing intention, not promised generation accuracy; exact frame timing requires verification or an authorized editing pass. Keep any flash brief enough that it does not hide the outcome, and re-establish persistent effects after it.
